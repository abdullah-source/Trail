# Professional waitlists: researchers, lawyers, writers

Purpose: collect intent and learn, not sell. Trail today is a Chrome extension and web app built for students. Nothing here promises a feature, a date or a price to professionals. Each group joins the waitlist with its own `source` (`researcher`, `lawyer`, `writer`) from the "Who it's for" band on the home page; these sign-ups count toward the 500 (target: 20 across the three, see `PLAN.md`).

The regulatory facts below come from the research in the approved plan. Read the primary source (the notice, the bill, the article, the order) before quoting any of it to an interviewee or in public.

How to run each interview:
- 20 minutes, video or phone. Ask about the past ("the last time you..."), not about what they would do with a product.
- No pitch until the last 3 minutes, and only if they ask. When describing Trail, use the same facts as everywhere else: it records how you write on your own device, signs a fingerprint every 10 minutes into a public append-only log, lets anyone verify an exported record, never detects AI, never scores, and the server never sees the text.
- End with: "Who else should I talk to?" Ask for two names.
- Log every interview in the table at the end within an hour.

Outreach message for all three groups (adapt the first line):

> Hi [name], I'm Abdullah Ali, a student at Harvey Mudd in the Claremont accelerator. I'm researching how [researchers / lawyers / writers] are handling new rules on disclosing AI use, specifically [NIH's notice on AI-developed applications / California SB 574 and judges' AI-disclosure orders / the EU AI Act's disclosure rule and false AI accusations]. I'm not selling anything. Could I ask you about your experience for 20 minutes this week or next? Happy to share what I learn from the other interviews.

## 1. Researchers and grant writers

NIH notice NOT-OD-25-132 says NIH will not consider applications that were substantially developed by AI to be the original ideas of the applicants, and that NIH can act on this after an award has been made. The notice also says NIH will use technology to identify AI-generated content, which means a researcher could face a question about an application they wrote themselves, with only the final text as evidence. Researchers already write in tools that keep some history (Word, Google Docs, Overleaf), but that history is not something a program officer or a research-integrity office can verify independently, and most applications have several authors. The question for these interviews is whether researchers want a verifiable record of how an application was written, who in the process (PI, co-investigators, grant writers, research-development offices) feels the risk, and whether the same student-owned, text-never-leaves-the-device approach fits multi-author work. This group shares a channel with the student product: the same universities, often the same people a few years later.

**Five discovery questions**
1. Tell me about the last grant application you wrote. Who wrote which parts, and in what tools?
2. Has NOT-OD-25-132, or a similar rule from a journal or another funder, changed anything about how you or your lab write? What, specifically?
3. Have you, or anyone you know, been asked whether AI was used in an application or paper? What happened, and what did they have to show?
4. When you use AI tools now (for literature, code, language editing), how do you keep track of that, if at all?
5. If a program officer asked tomorrow how a section was written, what would you send them?

**Where to find 10 people**
1. NIH RePORTER: search for funded investigators at the Claremont colleges (Harvey Mudd, Pomona, Keck Graduate Institute, Claremont Graduate University) and email 5.
2. Graduate students and postdocs writing F31, F32 or K applications: ask friends in labs at nearby universities for introductions.
3. Research-development or sponsored-programs offices: ask a staff member who runs grant-writing workshops for 20 minutes of their view (they see many applicants).
4. Grant Professionals Association members and LinkedIn searches for "NIH grant writer" or "grant development".
5. r/AskAcademia and r/labrats: ask a question in a post that follows the rules, then ask commenters with experience if they'd talk.

## 2. Lawyers (litigators, California first)

California SB 574, signed on 30 September 2026 and effective 1 January 2027, is the first state law requiring lawyers to disclose AI use in court filings and to personally verify every citation. Federal judges have standing orders that go further in places: some ask which portions of a filing used AI, and some ask for records of prompts. There have been about 1,240 US sanction instances involving AI misuse in filings to date, and *Johnson v. Dunn* (2025) showed that a firm's written AI policy, without proof it was followed, did not protect it. Lawyers need a record of what they did that does not expose privileged text, and that consists of facts rather than a score that opposing counsel could attack. Trail's design (the server never sees the text; the record is facts, signed and verifiable) fits that, but lawyers write in Word, not a browser, so these interviews are discovery only: what a disclosure actually needs to say, who in a firm prepares it, and what a judge would accept. Target: California solo and small-firm litigators before 1 January 2027.

**Five discovery questions**
1. Walk me through the last filing you prepared: who drafted it, in what tools, and who checked the citations and how?
2. What has your firm done so far about SB 574 or judges' AI-disclosure orders? Who owns that?
3. Have you seen a disclosure or certification that a judge questioned or rejected? What was missing?
4. If a judge asked you which portions of a filing used AI, how would you answer today, and how long would it take?
5. What would you never allow a tool to see or store, and who in the firm would have to approve a new tool?

**Where to find 10 people**
1. 5C and Harvey Mudd alumni who practise law in California (alumni directory, LinkedIn, the accelerator's mentor list).
2. California Lawyers Association, Solo and Small Firm Section: events and member directory.
3. Los Angeles County Bar Association and nearby county bar associations: legal-technology or AI committees and their events.
4. LinkedIn: California litigators who have posted about SB 574 or AI sanctions.
5. Law-school clinics and legal-writing instructors at nearby law schools, who can introduce recent graduates now in small firms.

## 3. Professional writers, journalists and publishers

From 2 August 2026, Article 50(4) of the EU AI Act requires that AI-generated or AI-manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest be disclosed as such, unless the text has gone through human review or editorial control and a person or organisation holds editorial responsibility. That exemption depends on being able to show the human review happened. In the US, the Copyright Office requires applicants to disclose AI-generated material in a registration. Meanwhile freelance writers are losing clients and payments after a client runs their work through a detector, with nothing to show in return except their word. Writers would want what students want: a record they own, showing how they wrote, that a client or editor can verify without an account and without the writer's drafts leaving their machine. The interviews should find out who asks writers for proof today (clients, editors, platforms), what format editors would accept, and whether publishers would want a contributor to attach a record to a submission.

**Five discovery questions**
1. Has a client or editor ever questioned whether you wrote something yourself? What happened, and what did you show them?
2. What do your contracts or contributor guidelines now say about AI? Who wrote that, and has anyone checked it?
3. (Editors and publishers) How do you show that a piece went through human editorial review today? Would you need to show it to a regulator or a reader?
4. Which tools do you draft in, and what history do they keep?
5. When you have lost work or payment over an AI question, what did it cost you?

**Where to find 10 people**
1. Freelance writers who have posted publicly about being falsely accused of using AI (LinkedIn, X, Medium, Substack): message 5 directly.
2. r/freelanceWriters: a question post that follows the rules, then follow up with people who describe a real incident.
3. Editorial Freelancers Association and similar writers' organisations: events and member forums.
4. Editors at EU-based newsrooms and publishers who have written about AI policy (LinkedIn), for Article 50(4).
5. Student journalists and editors at The Student Life and other 5C publications, who are a step from professional newsrooms and can introduce alumni.
